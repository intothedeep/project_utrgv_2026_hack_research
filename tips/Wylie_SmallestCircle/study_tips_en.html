The smallest circle that meets three lines in three dimensions: study tips

This is a study map for beginners. You learn what to know and why, step by step. Tab 1 has 6 steps. Tabs 2 and 3 have 5 steps each. Each step builds on the one before.

The problem is this. Three infinite lines are given in space. Find the smallest circle (center, radius, plane) that meets all three lines.

Step 1 of 6

Problem and key idea: think of the circle as part of a sphere

Problem to solve. Three lines can sit in many layouts. Solving each case alone never ends. We need one question that covers all cases.

Lower bound. Say a circle with center c and radius r meets line L at point p. Then this inequality holds.

dist(c, L) ≤ |c − p| = r

So every circle that meets the condition satisfies this.

r ≥ r* = minc maxi dist(c, Li)

r* is the radius of the smallest sphere that meets all three lines. The distance from a point to a line is a convex function. The maximum of three convex functions is also convex. So this is a convex optimization in c. Add the variable r. Then it is a second-order cone programming (SOCP) problem with 4 variables (c, r).

The lower bound is always reached. Say one pair is not parallel. The distance grows as c moves away, so a minimum exists. If all three lines are parallel, reduce to a cross-section. At the optimum, the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions (KKT) hold. They say a positive combination of the unit vectors ui is 0. Each ui points from a contact point to c. If r* = 0, ui is undefined. So this argument holds only for r* > 0.

Three such vectors must lie in one plane. So the contact points and c lie in the same plane. That plane gives a great circle that passes through all three lines.

Say two constraints are active. Then the two contact points are antipodal points of the sphere. Then you can rotate the plane to catch the third line.

Conclusion: the case analysis shrinks to one decision, "are 2 or 3 lines active?", and a solution of the 3-active subcase. An active line is a line whose multiplier in the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions (KKT) is positive. That line is at distance exactly r from the optimal center.

Step 6 of 6

Study tips and plan

Problem to solve. Time to the deadline is short. You save time if you know what to learn first and where mistakes are likely.

Basics to learn first

  1. Line forms: vector form a + t·d, two-point form, Plücker coordinates
  2. Derive the point-line distance and the foot of perpendicular, do not memorize them
  3. Decide how two lines relate: d₁×d₂, (a₂−a₁)·(d₁×d₂)
  4. The common perpendicular of two skew lines and the formula for the nearest points
  5. Incircle, circumcircle, and the minimum enclosing circle (MEC) for acute and obtuse triangles
  6. Convex function, minimax, Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions (KKT), and the subgradient of a max
  7. Apollonius point-line-line problem (PLL): the circle tangent to 1 point and 2 lines
  8. Second-order cone programming (SOCP): min r s.t. ‖Pi(c − ai)‖ ≤ r

Study order

  1. Redraw the figures of Figure 1 in the problem statement by hand. Write the number of parallel, meeting, and skew pairs
  2. Write the lower bound proof yourself in 5 lines
  3. Write the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions (KKT) argument: why is it coplanar if a positive combination is 0?
  4. Press every preset in the explorer above and tabulate the active counts
  5. Derive from easy to hard: triangle, 3 parallel, 2 parallel, perpendicular line, 1 cross, all skew
  6. Plan the check that compares the closed forms with second-order cone programming (SOCP) numerical solutions

Pitfalls

  • The meaning of "touch". Intersect and tangent give different answers in almost all cases, except the coplanar triangle. A 3-active solution also makes the line tangent to the sphere, but not to the circle. Example: Figure 1, acute prism. Ask the setter and state it in the paper.
  • Missing degenerate cases. Concurrent lines (r = 0) and two coincident lines.
  • The solution is not unique. Describe the family of solutions for parallel cases.
  • Obtuse prism. The circle plane is not perpendicular to the lines.
  • Tolerance. Deciding parallel and meeting needs ε. In the paper, assume exact arithmetic.
  • General skew case. There may be no closed form with radicals. Do not claim one without proof.

Questions to ask Tim Wylie first

  1. Does "touches" mean the circle intersects the line, or is tangent to it?
  2. Is r = 0 accepted as an answer when the three lines pass through one point?
  3. When the solution is not unique (parallel cases), is one circle enough? Or must we describe the whole family?
  4. Which input form is best for closed forms: vector form, two-point form, or Plücker coordinates?

Step 1 of 5

What this problem is

Problem to solve. You cannot search the literature for a problem without a name. A name links it to earlier work.

"The smallest circle that meets three lines" is an intersection radius problem in computational geometry. In recent terms, it is the circle version of the minimum touching ball problem.

The starting point is the minimum enclosing circle (MEC) that wraps a set of points. Think of it as that problem with lines in place of points, and a curve in place of a disk.

As geometry

Among the circles that share a point with all three lines, take the one with the smallest radius. You find the center, the radius, and the plane.

As optimization

It is minc maxi dist(c, Li). It is a 1-center problem that minimizes the largest distance to the lines. Second-order cone programming (SOCP) solves it.

As algebra

With 3 active lines, it is a system of three quadratics and one cubic. The degree of the solution and its Galois group decide whether a closed form exists.

Step 4 of 5

What it means in this context: why it matters

Problem to solve. We must explain why a closed form for just three lines has value. The answer is that it is a part of a larger algorithm.

1. A part of an algorithm

Say you solve the smallest intersecting sphere of n lines as a linear programming-type problem (LP-type problem). Then every step needs the exact smallest sphere of 3 or 4 lines. A closed form makes this inner loop fast and exact.

2. Robustness

Numerical solvers turn unstable on degenerate input such as parallel lines, meeting lines, or concurrent lines. With a case split and a formula per case, you can use exact arithmetic.

3. Circle versus sphere

The literature mostly treats spheres. For three lines, the smallest circle is a great circle of the smallest sphere. This is the key theorem of this page, and it bridges the two problems. It generally breaks for 4 or more lines. Example: 4 lines perpendicular to the radius at the 4 vertices of a regular tetrahedron on the unit sphere. The smallest sphere is then the unit sphere alone. The 4 contact points are not in one plane, so no great circle can be the answer.

Questions that follow from this problem (hypotheses)

  • With 4 or more lines, the great circle theorem generally breaks. Example: 4 tangent lines of a regular tetrahedron have contact points that do not lie in one plane. There is no reason for 4 active constraints to lie in one plane.
  • Then it is open whether the smallest circle problem is a linear programming-type problem (LP-type problem).
  • With the tangent reading, the smallest circle differs in almost all cases, except the coplanar triangle. A 3-active solution also makes the line tangent to the sphere, but not to the circle.
  • The segment version must also treat contact at the endpoints.
  • We do not yet know the polynomial degree of the general skew lines case.
